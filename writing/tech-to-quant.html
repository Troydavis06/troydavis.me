Notes on the tech-to-quant pivot

Jan 2026 · 4 min read

A lot of CS students I know are interested in quant finance but aren’t sure what the actual overlap is with software engineering. I’ve been learning about it for the past year, so here’s what I’ve pieced together — not career advice, just observations.

The skills that transfer directly

Systems programming. Low-latency C++ is a real skill in HFT and the bar is high. If you can write cache-friendly code, reason about memory layout, and profile at the nanosecond level, that’s genuinely valuable. The same instincts that make you good at systems work make you good here.

Probability and statistics. Not the undergrad survey course version — the kind where you actually understand what a distribution is, when the CLT applies and when it doesn’t, and why naive backtesting is almost always lying to you. The math department matters here more than I expected.

Comfort with ambiguity. In software you usually know what correct looks like. In quant work, the signal is noisy, the ground truth is contested, and most ideas don’t work. The ability to run a principled experiment and not fool yourself about the results is more important than knowing any particular tool.

What’s actually different

The feedback loops are much longer. You ship a feature in SWE and you know in hours whether it works. You test a strategy and you’re waiting weeks or months for statistically meaningful results. That’s a different kind of patience.

The domain knowledge gap is real. Market microstructure — how prices actually form, what spreads mean, why order flow is informative — takes time to build intuition for. You can’t just read a textbook and have it click. You need to sit with real data and watch things happen.

The interview process at quant firms is also just different. More math olympiad-style problem solving, more probability puzzles, more “estimate this on the back of an envelope.” If that style of thinking comes naturally, it’s a good sign. If it doesn’t, that’s worth knowing early.

How I’ve been approaching it

Building things is still the best way I know to learn. The matching engine was motivated by wanting to understand how the order book works at a mechanical level, not just conceptually. Reading about it is fine. Implementing it is better.

I’ve also been spending time with market data — just loading tick data, plotting things, asking questions. Why does the spread widen here? What’s happening to volume at that price? A lot of it I can’t answer yet. But asking the right questions is usually the first step.

The honest summary: I’m early in this. These are notes, not conclusions. I’ll probably look back in two years and cringe at some of it. That seems fine.
